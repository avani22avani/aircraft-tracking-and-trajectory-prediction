0031`t[Heading1]`tABSTRACT
0032`t[]`tAutomatic Dependent Surveillanceâ€“Broadcast (ADS-B) is a cooperative surveillance technology that has rapidly become the backbone of modern air-traffic management. Every suitably equipped aircraft periodically broadcasts, on the internationally allocated frequency of 1090 MHz, self-determined state-vector information including its International Civil Aviation Organisation (ICAO) 24-bit address, callsign, GNSS-derived position, barometric altitude, ground speed and heading that can be received by any suitably equipped ground station within line-of-sight range, without the need for interrogation by a radar. This project, â€œDesign and Implementation of an Real-Time Aircraft Tracking and Trajectory Prediction Systemâ€, presents the complete design, implementation and evaluation of an end-to-end software platform that ingests live Mode-S Extended Squitter (DF17) broadcasts captured by a uAvionix PingStation receiver front-end, decodes and validates them, stores the resulting flight-trajectory records in a relational database, predicts each aircraft's near-future track using great-circle projection geometry, and presents the resulting live air picture through an interactive browser-based dashboard built around the Leaflet.js mapping library and the Flask web-application framework.
0033`t[]`tThe work undertaken spans both the electronics/communication and the computer science aspects of the problem. On the radio-frequency side, the report examines the physical-layer characteristics of the 1090 MHz Mode-S Extended Squitter signal â€” its Pulse-Position-Modulation (PPM) encoding, preamble structure, 112-bit Downlink Format 17 (DF17) frame layout, CRC-based parity-integrity checking, and the radiation-pattern requirements of the receiving antenna and relates these physical-layer properties to the reliability and range of the acquired data. On the software side, the report documents a layered, three-tier architecture comprising a hardware/acquisition tier, an application/processing tier (the data-source selector, the PingStation pre-processor, and the FlightTrajectoryStore engine), and a presentation tier served by a RESTful Flask back-end and an interactive front-end.
0034`t[]`tThe system was tested using both live broadcast captures and simulated traffic scenarios over an evaluation period, and its performance was characterised in terms of message-decoding accuracy, geographic reception range, trajectory-prediction error, end-to-end latency and dashboard responsiveness. The results demonstrate that the implemented system reliably decodes Mode-S DF17 broadcasts at ranges consistent with the theoretical radio-horizon limits for the antenna height used, achieves a median trajectory-prediction error within an acceptable bound for a 30-minute prediction horizon, and sustains an update rate of approximately 1 Hz with sub-second end-to-end latency confirming its suitability as a low-cost, open, educational alternative to commercial flight-tracking platforms. The report concludes with a discussion of the limitations of the current implementation and recommends directions for future enhancement, including multilateration-based position validation, machine-learning-based trajectory prediction, and integration with additional surveillance data sources such as ASTERIX-encoded radar feeds.

0258`t[Heading2]`t1.1 General
0259`t[]`tAir-traffic surveillance, the continual process of determining the identity, position and intent of aircraft operating within a given volume of airspace, is one of the central pillars of safe and efficient aviation. For more than half a century this function was performed almost exclusively by ground-based radar: Primary Surveillance Radar (PSR), which detects the echo of a transmitted pulse reflected from an aircraft's airframe, and Secondary Surveillance Radar (SSR), which interrogates an on-board transponder and receives a coded reply containing identity and altitude information (Mode A/C, and later Mode S). While radar-based surveillance has served aviation well, it suffers from well-known limitations: it is expensive to install and maintain, its update rate is constrained by antenna rotation speed (typically 4â€“12 seconds per scan), its accuracy degrades with range due to beam spreading, and it provides only an external, ground-derived estimate of an aircraft's state rather than the aircraft's own, more precise, satellite-derived measurements.
0260`t[]`tAutomatic Dependent Surveillanceâ€“Broadcast (ADS-B) was conceived as a technology that would overcome these limitations by inverting the surveillance paradigm: instead of a ground station interrogating an aircraft, the aircraft itself periodically broadcasts â€” typically once or twice per second â€” a self-generated report of its own state vector, derived primarily from an on-board Global Navigation Satellite System (GNSS) receiver and other avionics sensors. The term is best understood by decomposing it: the surveillance is Automatic because it requires no pilot or controller action; it is Dependent because the quality of the reported position depends on the aircraft's own navigation sources; and it is Broadcast because the report is transmitted omnidirectionally to any suitably equipped receiver â€” whether mounted in another aircraft (for Airborne Collision Avoidance and self-separation), at an Air Navigation Service Provider's ground station, or, as is increasingly the case, in the hands of hobbyists, researchers and students operating low-cost Software-Defined Radio (SDR) receivers.
0261`t[]`tIn the international civil aviation context, ADS-B is mandated by regulators such as the United States Federal Aviation Administration (FAA), under its NextGen modernisation programme, and the European Union Aviation Safety Agency (EASA), and is standardised by the International Civil Aviation Organisation (ICAO) and by RTCA documents such as DO-260B. Two principal data links are used worldwide: the 1090 MHz Extended Squitter (1090ES), which re-uses the existing Mode-S transponder infrastructure and is the dominant link for air-transport category aircraft, and the 978 MHz Universal Access Transceiver (UAT) link used mainly by general-aviation aircraft in United States airspace below eighteen-thousand feet. This project concentrates on the 1090 MHz Extended Squitter link because of its global ubiquity and because low-cost receiver hardware for this band is widely available, a decisive practical consideration for an academic, laboratory-scale implementation.
0262`t[]`tThe growing availability of inexpensive RTL-SDR dongles and purpose-built receiver front-ends, such as the uAvionix PingStation device used in this project â€” together with mature open-source decoding libraries, has placed what was once specialised aerospace instrumentation within reach of undergraduate laboratories. This presents a unique opportunity: a student project can, with a modest budget, capture *real* broadcast signals from aircraft passing overhead, decode them down to the bit level, and build a complete, end-to-end surveillance and analysis pipeline that mirrors â€” in miniature â€” the architecture of operational air-traffic-management systems. It is precisely this opportunity that motivated the present work.
0263`t[Heading2]`t1.2  Motivation
0264`t[]`tThree considerations motivated the choice of this project topic. First, ADS-B sits at the intersection of electronics and communication engineering and computer science: understanding it requires familiarity with radio-frequency propagation, modulation and coding theory, digital-signal processing and antenna design on the one hand, and with software architecture, databases, web technologies and data visualisation on the other. A project built around ADS-B therefore offers an unusually rich platform for demonstrating the integration of theory from across the Electronics & Communication Engineering curriculum â€” RF and microwave engineering, digital communication, antenna theory, embedded systems, and computer networks â€” within a single, coherent system.
0265`t[]`tSecond, live aircraft-tracking systems possess an immediate, tangible appeal: the data is real, it is continuously generated by aircraft that can often be seen or heard overhead, and the resulting visualisation â€” a moving map populated with aircraft icons, callsigns, altitudes and projected tracks â€” provides instant, intuitive feedback on whether the underlying decoding and processing pipeline is functioning correctly. This makes the system both an effective demonstration of engineering competence and a genuinely useful tool that can be operated long after the academic submission deadline has passed.
0266`t[]`tThird, by building the system from first principles â€” writing the decoder logic, the database schema, the trajectory-prediction mathematics and the web dashboard rather than simply deploying an existing turnkey package â€” the project offers maximal opportunity for learning. Each subsystem (RF front-end characterisation, frame decoding, data validation, persistent storage, predictive analytics and interactive visualisation) exercises a distinct skill set, and the integration of these subsystems into a single coherent application demonstrates systems-engineering competence that a narrower, single-topic project would not.

0276`t[Heading2]`t1.4  Scope of the Project
0277`t[]`tThe scope of the present work is deliberately bounded so as to remain achievable within the time and resource constraints of an undergraduate project while still constituting a complete, functioning system. The implementation focuses exclusively on the 1090 MHz Extended-Squitter downlink (DF17/DF18 air-borne position, velocity and identification messages); the 978 MHz UAT link, Mode-S surveillance replies (DF4/DF5/DF20/DF21) and Traffic-Information-Service-Broadcast (TIS-B) messages are outside the scope of the implementation, although they are discussed where relevant for context. Position validation is performed using the single-receiver decoding techniques available in the open literature (CPR decoding using either globally or locally unambiguous position pairs); multilateration using multiple, geographically distributed receivers â€” which would allow independent verification of a broadcast position â€” is identified as a direction for future work rather than implemented in the present system. Similarly, the trajectory-prediction algorithm implemented uses deterministic great-circle projection from the last known state vector; statistical or machine-learning-based prediction models that incorporate historical manoeuvre patterns are discussed in Chapter 5 as potential extensions but were not implemented for the present submission.
0278`t[]`tOn the software side, the system is implemented as a single-process Flask application intended for deployment on a single host machine (a laboratory PC or a small single-board computer); horizontal scaling, multi-tenant deployment and production-grade security hardening are not within scope, although the architecture has been designed so that such extensions would not require a fundamental redesign. The user interface targets modern desktop and mobile web browsers; native mobile applications are not provided.

0284`t[Heading2]`t2.1  Evolution of Aircraft Surveillance Technology
0285`t[]`tThe history of aircraft surveillance is, in large part, the history of radar. Primary Surveillance Radar (PSR), developed during the Second World War, determines an aircraft's range and bearing by measuring the time-of-flight and angle-of-arrival of a reflected microwave pulse; it requires no cooperation from the aircraft and can therefore detect non-cooperative or non-transponder-equipped targets, but it cannot directly determine an aircraft's identity or altitude, and its performance is strongly affected by the target's radar cross-section, by weather (ground clutter, precipitation) and by terrain masking. Secondary Surveillance Radar (SSR), introduced in the 1950s, addressed the identity problem by having the ground station transmit a coded interrogation pulse train that triggers an on-board transponder to reply with an encoded identity (Mode A) and, later, pressure altitude (Mode C). Mode S â€” standardised by ICAO in the 1980s â€” extended this scheme with selective addressing (each aircraft is assigned a unique 24-bit ICAO address), reducing the "garbling" that occurred when multiple aircraft replied simultaneously, and introduced a general-purpose data-link capability that could carry richer information than the simple identity/altitude codes of Modes A and C [1], [2].
0286`t[]`tIt is this Mode-S data-link capability that ADS-B exploits. Rather than waiting to be interrogated, a Mode-S transponder configured for Extended-Squitter operation periodically and spontaneously transmits â€” "squitters" â€” a 112-bit message (Downlink Format 17) containing, among other things, the aircraft's GNSS-derived position (encoded using the Compact Position Reporting, or CPR, scheme), barometric or geometric altitude, velocity vector, and identification/category information [3]. Because this information originates on board the aircraft and is derived from satellite navigation rather than from a ground-based measurement, its accuracy is frequently superior to that obtainable from radar â€” a major factor behind the international decision to mandate ADS-B Out equipage on commercial and, increasingly, general-aviation aircraft [4], [5].
0287`t[Heading2]`t2.2  ADS-B Standards and the Regulatory Landscape
0288`t[]`tThe technical specification of the 1090 MHz Extended-Squitter ADS-B link is documented in RTCA DO-260 and its subsequent revisions DO-260A and DO-260B, which define the message formats, encoding rules, and the Minimum Operational Performance Standards (MOPS) that avionics equipment must satisfy [6]. ICAO Annex 10, Volume IV, provides the international Standards and Recommended Practices (SARPs) for Mode-S and ADS-B, ensuring that equipment built to different national specifications remains globally interoperable [2]. On the regulatory side, the United States Federal Aviation Administration's ADS-B Out mandate (effective 1 January 2020) requires aircraft operating in defined categories of airspace to broadcast ADS-B Out messages compliant with DO-260B, as part of the wider NextGen air-traffic-modernisation programme [7]. The European Union, through EASA and the Single European Sky ATM Research (SESAR) programme, has issued analogous requirements for ADS-B-equipage in European airspace [8]. These mandates have ensured that an overwhelming majority of commercial air transport aircraft, and a rapidly growing fraction of general-aviation aircraft, now broadcast ADS-B continuously â€” which is precisely what makes a student-built ADS-B receiver project both feasible and rewarding: there is, quite literally, always live data to receive.
0289`t[]`tIt is worth noting, for completeness, that a second ADS-B data link exists: the 978 MHz Universal Access Transceiver (UAT) link, used predominantly by general-aviation aircraft operating below eighteen-thousand feet within United States airspace, as an alternative to the 1090ES link for aircraft that do not already carry a Mode-S transponder [9]. Because 1090 MHz Extended-Squitter is the link used worldwide by air-transport category aircraft, and because suitable receiver hardware for this band is comparatively easy to obtain, this project restricts its scope to the 1090ES link, as discussed in Section 1.4.
0290`t[Heading2]`t2.3  Existing Open-Source and Commercial ADS-B Platforms
0291`t[]`tA vibrant open-source ecosystem has grown up around ADS-B reception, driven largely by the wide availability of inexpensive RTL2832U-based USB software-defined-radio dongles. Foremost among the open-source decoders is dump1090, originally written by Salvatore Sanfilippo and subsequently forked and extended by numerous contributors (notably the FlightAware and readsb variants), which demodulates the raw I/Q samples from an RTL-SDR device, detects Mode-S preambles, decodes DF17/DF18 Extended-Squitter frames, and exposes the resulting aircraft table through both a textual interface and a built-in web map [10]. Crowd-sourced aggregation networks such as FlightAware, ADS-B Exchange and the academically-oriented OpenSky Network collect feeds from thousands of volunteer-operated ground stations worldwide and republish the combined picture â€” OpenSky, in particular, also makes large historical datasets available for research purposes and has published extensively on data-quality and coverage characteristics of crowd-sourced ADS-B networks [11], [12]. Commercial platforms such as FlightRadar24 and PlaneFinder provide polished, consumer-facing visualisations built on similar underlying decoding pipelines, often supplemented with proprietary radar and schedule data to fill coverage gaps [13].
0292`t[]`tThe Python ecosystem additionally provides decoding libraries such as pyModeS, which implements the bit-level decoding rules for the various Downlink Formats and Extended-Squitter sub-types in a well-documented, research-friendly form, and has been used as a reference for verifying the correctness of the decoding logic implemented in the present project [14]. Hardware vendors such as uAvionix have, more recently, brought to market purpose-built receiver appliances â€” including the PingStation device used as the RF front-end for this project â€” that perform the demodulation and framing in dedicated firmware and expose the decoded messages as a structured hexadecimal log stream over a simple interface, considerably simplifying the acquisition stage of a project such as this one [15]. The present work draws on the architectural lessons of dump1090 and pyModeS while adopting the PingStation as an acquisition front-end, and builds, on top of that foundation, an original storage, prediction and visualisation layer tailored to the project's objectives.
0293`t[Heading2]`t2.4  Approaches to Flight-Trajectory Prediction
0294`t[]`tPredicting an aircraft's future position from its currently observed state is a long-standing problem in air-traffic management, with applications ranging from short-term conflict detection to strategic flow management. The simplest class of techniques sometimes called "dead-reckoning" or "linear extrapolation" methods projects the aircraft's position forward in time by assuming that its current ground speed and heading remain constant; on the spherical Earth, this projection is most correctly performed along a great-circle path using standard geodesic forward-projection formulas (the "direct geodesic problem") [16]. Such methods are computationally inexpensive and require no information beyond the most recent state vector, but their accuracy degrades over longer horizons because real flights are rarely perfectly straight: aircraft climb, descend, change heading to follow airway structure, and are vectored by air-traffic control.
0295`t[]`tMore sophisticated approaches incorporate additional information to improve accuracy over longer horizons. Flight-plan-based methods use the aircraft's filed route and published airway/waypoint structure to constrain the predicted path; trajectory-clustering and case-based-reasoning methods match the observed initial segment of a flight against a database of historically observed trajectories between the same originâ€“destination pair; and, most recently, machine-learning approaches â€” including recurrent neural networks, sequence-to-sequence models and Gaussian-process regression â€” have been applied to learn manoeuvre patterns directly from large historical trajectory datasets such as those published by the OpenSky Network [17], [18]. These data-driven methods can achieve markedly better accuracy over horizons of tens of minutes, but they require substantial training data, computational resources for model training, and careful validation â€” requirements that lie beyond the scope of an undergraduate project of this size and duration.
0296`t[]`tGiven these considerations, the present project adopts a great-circle, constant-velocity projection model â€” implemented using standard forward-geodesic formulas applied to the most recently decoded state vector of each tracked aircraft â€” as a pragmatic, well-understood and easily verifiable baseline. Section 3.6 documents the mathematical basis of this model in detail, and Chapter 4 quantitatively evaluates its prediction error over a thirty-minute horizon; Chapter 5 discusses how the more sophisticated, data-driven approaches surveyed above could be incorporated as a future enhancement.
0297`t[Heading2]`t2.5  Summary and Identification of the Research Gap
0298`t[]`tThe literature surveyed above shows that, while both the underlying ADS-B technology and a number of mature open-source decoding tools are well documented, there are comparatively few published, complete, end-to-end *educational* reference implementations that integrate â€” within a single, coherently engineered codebase â€” RF-front-end acquisition, Extended-Squitter decoding, persistent storage, trajectory prediction and an interactive visual dashboard, while also documenting, at an undergraduate level of detail, the relevant electronics-and-communication theory that underpins each stage. Existing tools such as dump1090 and pyModeS are excellent at the decoding stage but provide little in the way of persistent storage, predictive analytics or polished visualisation; commercial platforms provide polished visualisation but are closed-source and document neither their internal architecture nor the underlying RF theory. This project addresses that gap by building, end to end, a complete and openly documented system the work described in the chapters that follow that can additionally serve as a teaching and demonstration platform for future students of Electronics & Communication Engineering.

0310`t[Heading2]`t3.2  The 1090 MHz Mode-S Extended-Squitter Physical Layer
0311`t[]`tBecause this is a project in Electronics & Communication Engineering, particular care was taken to understand â€” not merely to use â€” the physical-layer characteristics of the signal being received. This section documents that understanding, since it directly informed several implementation decisions (for example, the message-validation logic described in Section 3.4 relies on knowledge of the exact frame structure and the cyclic-redundancy-check convention described below).
0312`t[Heading3]`t3.2.1  Frequency Allocation and Link Budget Considerations
0313`t[]`tThe Extended-Squitter downlink operates on a single, internationally allocated carrier frequency of 1090 MHz, within the L-band of the radio spectrum. This frequency was originally chosen for Mode-S because it coincides with the reply frequency already used by legacy Mode A/C transponders, allowing Mode-S equipment to be backward compatible with the existing secondary-radar infrastructure. At this frequency, propagation is essentially line-of-sight: the signal does not diffract significantly around the curvature of the Earth or around large obstructions such as hills, and the maximum theoretical reception range is therefore governed by the radio-horizon distance, which depends on the height of both the transmitting (airborne) and receiving (ground-station) antennas above the local terrain. For a ground station at height h_r (in metres) and an aircraft at height h_t, the combined radio-horizon distance, in kilometres, may be approximated by the well-known relation d â‰ˆ 3.57 (âˆšh_r + âˆšh_t); for a typical laboratory rooftop installation at roughly 20 m above the surrounding terrain, and a cruising aircraft at 10,000 m, this evaluates to a theoretical maximum range of approximately 16 + 357 â‰ˆ 373 km, a figure broadly consistent with the reception ranges reported in Chapter 4.
0314`t[]`tIn practice, the achievable range is also a function of the transmitted power (Mode-S transponders typically radiate between 125 W and 500 W peak pulse power, depending on equipment class), the receiving antenna's gain and radiation pattern, feeder-cable losses, and the receiver's noise figure and sensitivity. The PingStation receiver front-end used in this project specifies a sensitivity of approximately âˆ’84 dBm for reliable Extended-Squitter decoding, which, when combined with a typical co-linear/discone antenna gain of around 3â€“5 dBi and modest feeder losses, comfortably supports reception at line-of-sight ranges of several hundred kilometres for high-altitude aircraft.
0315`t[Heading3]`t3.2.2  Pulse-Position Modulation and the Preamble
0316`t[]`tThe Mode-S Extended-Squitter downlink uses a binary modulation scheme called Pulse-Position Modulation (PPM). Each data bit occupies a fixed time slot of 1 microsecond, which is itself divided into two half-microsecond chips; a logical ONE is represented by RF energy present in the first half and absent in the second half of the slot, while a logical ZERO is represented by the opposite pattern â€” energy absent in the first half and present in the second. This scheme has the useful property that every bit period contains exactly one rising and one falling edge of RF energy, which simplifies clock recovery at the receiver and makes the signal robust against simple amplitude-only interference, since the decoder can examine the *relative* energy in the two half-bit intervals rather than relying on an absolute amplitude threshold [3].
0317`t[]`tEach Extended-Squitter transmission begins with a fixed, 8-microsecond preamble consisting of four short pulses (each 0.5 microseconds long) at precisely defined offsets nominally at 0.0, 1.0, 3.5 and 4.5 microseconds from the start of the transmission followed by 7.5 microseconds with no pulses before the data block begins. This distinctive pulse pattern, illustrated in Figure 2.2, gives a receiver a highly reliable signature against which to correlate the incoming signal: a candidate preamble is declared only when pulses are observed at all four expected offsets and at no other intermediate offset, which provides good rejection of noise and of overlapping transmissions from other aircraft ("garbling"). Once a valid preamble has been detected, the receiver knows precisely where the 56-bit (short, DF11) or 112-bit (long, DF17/DF18) data block begins, and can proceed to demodulate it bit by bit using the PPM rule described above.
0319`t[FigCaption]`tFig. 2.1: Idealised Mode-S preamble waveform: four 0.5 Âµs pulses at fixed offsets (0, 1.0, 3.5 and 4.5 Âµs) precede the 56- or 112-bit data block.
0320`t[Heading3]`t3.2.3  The DF17 Extended-Squitter Frame Structure
0321`t[]`tDownlink Format 17 (DF17) is the Extended-Squitter format used by Mode-S transponders to spontaneously broadcast ADS-B state-vector information; it is, in effect, the format that carries the bulk of the data of interest to this project. A DF17 frame is 112 bits (14 bytes) long and is organised into five principal fields, illustrated in Figure 2.1: a 5-bit Downlink Format field (DF, always equal to binary 10001 = 17 for Extended Squitter), a 3-bit Capability field (CA, indicating the transponder's level of Mode-S capability and current status), a 24-bit ICAO Aircraft Address field (AA, the unique identifier permanently assigned to that specific airframe), a 56-bit Message field (ME, whose internal structure depends on the message *type code* carried in its first five bits and which carries the actual payload â€” position, velocity, identification, etc.), and a final 24-bit Parity/Interrogator-Identity field (PI) [3], [6].
0323`t[FigCaption]`tFig. 2.2: Bit-level structure of a 112-bit Mode-S DF17 Extended-Squitter frame, showing the DF, CA, AA, ME and PI fields and their bit widths.
0324`t[]`tUnlike many communication protocols, Mode-S does not append a separate trailer field carrying an explicit cyclic-redundancy check; instead, the 24-bit PI field is computed as the remainder obtained when the first 88 bits of the frame (DF + CA + AA + ME) are divided, using modulo-2 (XOR) arithmetic, by a fixed 25-bit generator polynomial, and this remainder is then bit-wise combined with the (zero, for Extended Squitter) interrogator identity. A receiver can therefore validate a decoded frame simply by repeating this computation over the received bits and comparing the result against the received PI field if they match (after accounting for the all-zero interrogator identity expected for spontaneous squitters), the frame is declared valid with very high confidence; if not, the frame is discarded as corrupted. This CRC-style integrity check was the basis of the message-validation logic implemented in the PingstationPreprocessor module described in Section 3.4.3, and explains why the decoded data ingested by the rest of the system can be trusted to a high degree without further error-correction processing.
0325`t[]`tThe internal structure of the 56-bit ME field depends on a 5-bit Type Code (TC) sub-field at its head: type codes 1â€“4 indicate Aircraft Identification and Category messages (carrying the flight's callsign, encoded six bits per character in a restricted alphabet); type codes 9â€“18 and 20â€“22 indicate Airborne Position messages (carrying CPR-encoded latitude/longitude and altitude); type code 19 indicates an Airborne Velocity message (carrying ground speed, track angle/heading and vertical rate); and several other type-code ranges are reserved for surface-position, status and operational messages that, while decoded where encountered, are of secondary importance to this project's objectives. The decoder implemented for this project (see Section 3.4.2) dispatches on this type-code sub-field to select the appropriate field-extraction logic for each message.
0326`t[Heading3]`t3.2.4  Compact Position Reporting (CPR)
0327`t[]`tA subtlety that any ADS-B decoder must address is that the 56-bit ME field is too small to carry an aircraft's full latitude and longitude at the resolution required (better than five metres). The Extended-Squitter specification therefore uses a scheme called Compact Position Reporting (CPR), which divides the surface of the Earth into a regular grid and encodes only the aircraft's position *within* the current grid cell, using 17 bits each for latitude and longitude. Because this encoding is ambiguous on its own the same encoded value could correspond to many different grid cells each position message additionally carries a single "odd/even" format bit, and transponders alternate between odd-format and even-format encodings on successive transmissions. A receiver that has captured one message of each type within a sufficiently short time window (so that the aircraft has not moved between the two grid resolutions) can combine the two encodings to resolve the ambiguity and recover an unambiguous *global* position; alternatively, if the receiver already has a recent, trusted reference position for the aircraft (for example, from a previous globally-decoded fix), it can use a computationally simpler *locally* unambiguous decoding of a single message [3]. The decoding pipeline implemented for this project uses the globally-unambiguous, odd/even paired-message technique as its primary method, with locally-unambiguous decoding relative to the aircraft's last known position used as a fallback when a matching paired message has not yet been observed mirroring the approach documented for reference decoders such as pyModeS [14].
0328`t[Heading3]`t3.2.5  Antenna Considerations and Radiation Pattern
0329`t[]`tBecause aircraft may appear at any azimuth and at elevation angles ranging from just above the horizon (for distant, high-altitude traffic) to nearly overhead (for aircraft passing close to the receiving site), the receiving antenna must provide a radiation pattern that is omnidirectional in azimuth and reasonably uniform over a wide range of elevation angles. Two antenna types are commonly used for this purpose: co-linear (collinear) dipole-array antennas, which stack several half-wave radiating elements in phase to achieve increased gain in the horizontal plane at the expense of vertical beamwidth, and discone antennas, which provide very wide bandwidth and a smoothly omnidirectional pattern with moderate gain. Figure 3.8 shows an idealised polar plot of the azimuthal radiation pattern expected of such an antenna: essentially circular (omnidirectional) in azimuth, which ensures that aircraft are received with comparable signal strength regardless of their bearing from the ground station.
0331`t[FigCaption]`tFig. 3.1: Idealised azimuthal (polar) radiation pattern of a co-linear / discone 1090 MHz receiving antenna, showing the near-omnidirectional coverage required for ADS-B ground-station reception.
0332`t[]`tThe antenna's gain, polarisation (vertical, to match the vertically polarised transmissions from aircraft transponders), voltage standing-wave ratio (VSWR) at 1090 MHz, and mounting height above surrounding obstructions all directly affect the achievable signal-to-noise ratio and hence the reception range and decoding success rate discussed quantitatively in Chapter 4. For the laboratory installation used in this project, the antenna was mounted as high as practicable on the building roof, with a short, low-loss coaxial feeder run to the PingStation receiver, in order to maximise the received signal level and minimise additional noise injected by the feeder system.

0368`t[Heading3]`t3.4.1  The PingStation Hexadecimal Log Stream
0369`t[]`tThe uAvionix PingStation device used as the RF front-end for this project performs the demodulation, preamble detection and bit-level framing described in Section 3.2 entirely in its own firmware, and exposes the result as a continuous stream of timestamped, hexadecimal-encoded Mode-S frames one line per received message written to a log file (observed by the data_source.py module, which tails this log in the same way that the Unix "tail -f" utility would). 0370`t[]`tEach line of the log carries a timestamp (used to order messages and to detect and discard stale or out-of-sequence data), the raw hexadecimal frame (28 hexadecimal characters for a 112-bit DF17 frame), and, where available, an indicator of received signal strength. 0371`t[Heading3]`t3.4.2  Frame Decoding
0372`t[]`tOn receipt of each hexadecimal frame, the decoding stage performs the following sequence of operations, mirroring the frame structure documented in Section 3.2.3: (1) the hexadecimal string is converted to a 112-bit binary value; (2) the 5-bit Downlink Format field is extracted and examined frames whose DF value is not 17 (or, for completeness, 18, which is used by certain non-transponder ADS-B-equipped vehicles) are set aside, since they fall outside the scope of this project as discussed in Section 1.4; (3) the 24-bit ICAO Aircraft Address is extracted and used as the primary key by which subsequent messages from the same airframe are associated with one another; (4) the 5-bit Type Code at the head of the 56-bit Message field is examined to determine which of the message-specific decoders (identification, airborne position, airborne velocity, etc.) should be invoked; and (5) the appropriate decoder extracts the semantically meaningful fields callsign characters, CPR-encoded latitude/longitude and altitude, or ground speed/track-angle/vertical-rate, as applicable and assembles them, together with the ICAO address and a UTC timestamp, into a structured Python record that is passed onward to the preprocessing stage.
0373`t[]`tTwo points are worth emphasising about this design. First, decoding is performed *incrementally*: because no single DF17 message carries an aircraft's complete state (a position message, for instance, carries no velocity information, and vice versa), the system maintains, for each tracked ICAO address, a small in-memory "current state" record that is progressively updated as successive messages of different types are received and merged a design pattern directly analogous to the way an air-traffic controller's radar display is continuously refreshed by a stream of partial updates rather than complete state snapshots. Second, decoding is performed *defensively*: every extracted field is subject to a plausibility check (for example, an altitude value outside the range realistically achievable by civil aircraft, or a ground speed in excess of several thousand knots, is treated as a decoding error rather than accepted at face value) before being admitted to the preprocessing stage described next.
0374`t[Heading3]`t3.4.3  Validation and Normalisation (PingstationPreprocessor)
0375`t[]`tRaw decoded fields are not, in general, in a form directly suitable for storage or display: altitudes may be reported in feet while the rest of the system works in metres, speeds may be reported in knots while charts are more naturally expressed in kilometres per hour, timestamps may carry local-time or device-clock offsets that must be normalised to Coordinated Universal Time (UTC), and most importantly from a communication-engineering standpoint every frame must be checked for integrity before its contents are trusted. This last function is performed by repeating, in software, the modulo-2 polynomial-division computation described in Section 3.2.3 over the first 88 bits of each received frame and comparing the result with the received 24-bit Parity/Interrogator-Identity field; frames that fail this check are discarded immediately, ensuring that corrupted or partially received messages which do occur, particularly at the extremities of the reception range or in the presence of overlapping ("garbled") transmissions from multiple aircraft cannot propagate erroneous data into the trajectory store. 0405`t[]`tTwo practical refinements were added to this baseline model. First, the model is re-evaluated every time a new state-vector observation is received for an aircraft, so that the projected track is continuously "snapped back" onto the aircraft's most recent true heading and speed rather than drifting away from reality as a single, never-updated projection would; the displayed projection therefore always represents "where the aircraft will be in N minutes if it continues on its *currently observed* course and speed", which is both an honest and a useful statement to present to a user. Second, a simple confidence/staleness indicator is attached to each prediction, based on the age of the underlying observation and on the variability of the aircraft's recent heading â€” a track that has been observed to be turning is flagged as carrying a wider uncertainty than one that has been observed to be straight and level â€” providing the user with an intuitive cue as to how much weight to place on the displayed projection. Chapter 4 quantifies the resulting prediction error against the subsequently observed ground truth.
0406`t[Heading2]`t3.7  Aircraft Classification Logic
0407`t[]`tIn addition to displaying raw state-vector data, the system attempts to classify each tracked aircraft into one of three broad categories â€” helicopter, propeller-driven aeroplane, or jet/airliner-type aeroplane â€” so that an appropriate marker icon (helicopter.svg, propplane.svg or airplane.svg) can be displayed on the map. This classification draws on several complementary sources of evidence: the Mode-S "aircraft category" sub-field carried in identification messages (which, where present, directly distinguishes broad classes such as light aircraft, rotorcraft and large aircraft); the aircraft-type information returned by the external hexdb.io look-up service keyed on the ICAO address (which, where available, gives a definitive type designation); and, as a fallback when neither of the foregoing is available, simple heuristics based on the observed flight envelope â€” for example, an aircraft observed maintaining a very low ground speed while changing altitude rapidly and erratically is more consistent with rotorcraft behaviour than with fixed-wing behaviour, while one observed maintaining high cruise speed at high altitude is more consistent with a jet airliner than with a light propeller aircraft. Figure 3.6 presents this classification logic as a flowchart, showing how the available evidence is combined, in order of decreasing reliability, to arrive at a final classification (with a generic "unknown/default" icon used when no source yields a confident result).
0409`t[FigCaption]`tFig. 3.7: Flowchart of the aircraft-classification logic: Mode-S category code and external type look-up are tried first, with simple flight-envelope heuristics used as a fallback.
0410`t[]`tIt is important to be candid about the limitations of this approach: the heuristic fallback is, by its nature, imperfect, and can occasionally mis-classify an aircraft operating outside its "typical" flight envelope (for example, a light aircraft conducting low-speed circuit training, or a helicopter conducting a high-speed transit leg). The classification is therefore presented to the user as an informative *aid* â€” primarily affecting which marker icon is shown â€” rather than as an authoritative statement of aircraft type; where a definitive type designation is available from the external look-up service, it is always preferred over, and displayed alongside, the heuristic classification.
0411`t[Heading2]`t3.8  User-Interface Design
0412`t[]`tThe browser-based front end was designed around three design goals: immediacy (the user should be able to glance at the screen and immediately understand the current air picture), drill-down (the user should be able to select any individual aircraft and obtain detailed information about it without losing the overall picture), and responsiveness (the interface should remain smooth and usable on both desktop and mobile-sized displays, and should update frequently enough to feel "live"). Figure 3.6 (wireframe) sketches the resulting layout: a full-window interactive map (rendered using the Leaflet.js library against an open base-map tile layer) showing the current position of every tracked aircraft as a rotated marker icon corresponding to its classification (Section 3.7) and heading; a search/selection control allowing the user to locate a specific aircraft by callsign or ICAO address; and a bottom information panel that, when an aircraft is selected, displays its callsign, ICAO address, aircraft type, current altitude/speed/heading, a representative photograph (retrieved through the aircraft_lookup.py enrichment module), and a thumbnail summary chart.
0414`t[FigCaption]`tFig. 3.8: Wireframe sketch of the browser-based dashboard: full-window live map, search/selection control, and bottom aircraft-information panel with photograph and key state parameters.
0415`t[]`tThe client polls the Flask server's REST API (principally the /api/aircraft endpoint, which returns the current state of every actively tracked aircraft as a JSON array) at a fixed interval of approximately one second â€” a rate chosen to match the non-functional "1 Hz update" requirement identified in Section 3.1 while remaining light enough not to overload either the server or the client's rendering pipeline. On each poll, the client reconciles the returned aircraft list against the markers currently present on the map: existing markers are smoothly repositioned and re-rotated to their new state, markers for aircraft that have left the coverage area are removed, and markers for newly appeared aircraft are created â€” all without redrawing the underlying map tiles, which keeps the visual experience smooth even when dozens of aircraft are simultaneously tracked. When an aircraft is selected, a second endpoint (/api/aircraft/info) is queried to retrieve the richer, enrichment-augmented information shown in the bottom panel, and the projected-track overlay described in Section 3.6 is drawn ahead of the selected aircraft's marker. Analytical charts (of the kind presented in Chapter 4) are rendered using the Chart.js library, drawing on historical data served through the same REST API.

0448`t[Heading2]`t4.1  Test Setup and Data-Collection Methodology
0449`t[]`tThe system was evaluated using two complementary sources of data. The first was a series of live-reception sessions, each of several hours' duration, conducted with the PingStation receiver and co-linear antenna installed on the laboratory rooftop and connected to the host PC running the full software pipeline; these sessions captured genuine Mode-S Extended-Squitter broadcasts from commercial, cargo and general-aviation traffic overflying the region. 0472`t[Heading2]`t4.3  Message-Decoding Accuracy and Signal-Quality Analysis
0473`t[]`tTo characterise the reliability of the decoding pipeline, the fraction of received frames that passed the CRC-based integrity check described in Section 3.2.3 / 3.4.3 was recorded as a function of the estimated received signal strength. 0520`t[Heading2]`t4.7  Discussion of Results
0521`t[]`tTaken together, the results presented in this chapter indicate that the system meets the functional and non-functional requirements set out in Section 3.1: it reliably acquires and decodes live Mode-S Extended-Squitter broadcasts at ranges consistent with the theoretical line-of-sight limits for the installation's antenna height; it validates received frames with a pass-rate consistent with the underlying signal-to-noise conditions and rejects corrupted data before it can contaminate the trajectory store; it predicts future aircraft positions with an error that, while growing with the prediction horizon as expected for a deterministic model, remains useful for its intended illustrative purpose; and it sustains an update experience and end-to-end latency that comfortably satisfy the "feels live" design goal. At the same time, the results point candidly to the principal directions in which the system could be most usefully improved â€” most notably, reducing the contribution of client polling to overall latency, and improving long-horizon trajectory-prediction accuracy through more sophisticated, data-driven models â€” both of which are taken up as recommendations for future work in Chapter 5.

0525`t[Heading2]`t5.1  Summary of Work Carried Out
0526`t[]`tThis project set out to design, implement and evaluate a complete, end-to-end ADS-B aircraft-tracking and trajectory-prediction system, spanning the full chain from radio-frequency acquisition at 1090 MHz through to an interactive, browser-based visual dashboard. In pursuit of this goal, the project: studied and documented the physical- and link-layer characteristics of the Mode-S Extended-Squitter signal â€” its Pulse-Position-Modulation encoding, preamble structure, 112-bit DF17 frame format, parity-based integrity-checking convention, and Compact-Position-Reporting position-encoding scheme â€” and related these characteristics to concrete design decisions in the implementation; configured and characterised a low-cost RF front-end comprising a co-linear/discone 1090 MHz antenna and a uAvionix PingStation receiver; designed and implemented a layered, three-tier software architecture comprising a data-acquisition and decoding pipeline, a validation-and-normalisation stage built around a CRC-based frame-integrity check, a relational database (FlightTrajectoryStore) with a carefully designed logical schema, a great-circle, constant-velocity trajectory-prediction engine grounded in spherical-trigonometric (direct-geodesic) projection formulas, an aircraft-classification module combining Mode-S category codes, external type look-ups and flight-envelope heuristics, and an interactive Leaflet.js/Chart.js/Flask-based web dashboard; and, finally, evaluated the resulting system experimentally along four axes â€” reception range, decoding accuracy, trajectory-prediction error, and end-to-end latency/throughput â€” and compared the results against figures reported in the literature for comparable open-source and commercial platforms.
0527`t[]`tEvery objective set out in Section 1.3 was therefore addressed: the relevant RF theory was studied and documented; the front-end was set up and characterised; the decoding, storage, prediction and visualisation pipeline was designed, implemented and integrated into a single, coherent application; and the resulting system was evaluated quantitatively and discussed in relation to the wider field. The deliverable is a complete, self-contained, documented and (via PyInstaller) packaged application that can be operated, demonstrated and extended well beyond the duration of the academic project itself.
0528`t[Heading2]`t5.2  Conclusions
0529`t[]`tSeveral conclusions can be drawn from the work described in this report. First, the project confirms that a low-cost, single-station ADS-B receiver installation â€” built from readily available components and open-source software tooling â€” can achieve reception performance (range, update rate, decoding reliability) of the same general order as that reported for established open-source decoders, validating the choice of hardware and the correctness of the implemented decoding-and-validation pipeline. Second, the project demonstrates that a deterministic, physically-grounded trajectory-prediction model â€” requiring no training data and only a modest amount of spherical-trigonometric computation â€” can provide a useful, if imperfect, short-to-medium-term trajectory forecast, with error growing in a predictable and explicable manner as the prediction horizon lengthens. Third, the project shows that a thoughtfully layered software architecture â€” in which RF/decoding concerns, persistence concerns and presentation concerns are cleanly separated â€” makes it possible to build, test, evaluate and extend a system of this complexity within the time constraints of an undergraduate project, while keeping each individual component small enough to be fully understood and documented. Finally, and perhaps most importantly from an educational standpoint, the project demonstrates the value of building a system "from first principles": by implementing the decoding, validation, storage, prediction and visualisation logic directly, rather than simply deploying an existing turnkey package, the work produced not only a functioning system but also a deep, first-hand understanding of the electronics-and-communication principles â€” modulation, framing, error detection, antenna theory, link budgets and navigation geometry â€” that underlie modern cooperative air-traffic surveillance.
0530`t[Heading2]`t5.3  Limitations of the Present Implementation
0531`t[]`tIn the interest of completeness and academic honesty, it is important to record the limitations of the system as it currently stands, several of which were already foreshadowed in Sections 1.4, 3.7 and 4.7. The system relies on a single receiving station and therefore cannot independently verify a broadcast position through multilateration â€” it must trust the position reported by the aircraft itself (subject only to the frame-level integrity check described in Section 3.4.3), which means that a deliberately or accidentally mis-configured transponder could, in principle, cause an incorrect position to be displayed. The trajectory-prediction model is deterministic and "memoryless" â€” it has no knowledge of flight plans, airway structure, or an individual aircraft's historical manoeuvre patterns â€” and its accuracy therefore degrades, in an explicable but real way, over longer prediction horizons (Section 4.4). The aircraft-classification logic falls back, in the absence of definitive Mode-S category or external type-look-up information, on heuristics that can occasionally mis-classify aircraft operating outside their typical flight envelope (Section 3.7). The clientâ€“server update mechanism is currently based on periodic polling rather than a push-based notification scheme, which â€” as discussed in Section 4.5 â€” constitutes the dominant contribution to end-to-end latency. And, finally, the system is designed for single-host deployment and has not been evaluated under multi-user or high-density-traffic conditions significantly beyond those encountered during the evaluation sessions reported in Chapter 4.
0532`t[Heading2]`t5.4  Scope for Future Work
0533`t[]`tBuilding on the foundation established by this project, several directions for future enhancement can be identified, broadly in increasing order of implementation effort:
0534`t[]`tâ€¢  Multilateration-based position validation: by deploying additional, geographically distributed receiving stations and comparing the times of arrival of the same broadcast at each station, an independent estimate of an aircraft's position could be computed and cross-checked against its self-reported position â€” substantially increasing the trustworthiness of the displayed data and enabling detection of spoofed or malfunctioning transponders.
0535`t[]`tâ€¢  Push-based update delivery: replacing the current 1 Hz polling mechanism with a push-based protocol such as WebSockets or Server-Sent Events would eliminate the dominant contribution to end-to-end latency identified in Section 4.5, providing a noticeably more immediate "live" experience without requiring any change to the underlying decoding or storage pipeline.
0536`t[]`tâ€¢  Data-driven trajectory prediction: incorporating a machine-learning-based prediction model â€” for example, a recurrent neural network or sequence model trained on a large historical trajectory dataset such as that published by the OpenSky Network â€” could be expected to substantially reduce long-horizon prediction error by learning typical manoeuvre and approach/descent patterns, at the cost of a considerably more complex implementation, training and validation effort (Section 2.4).
0537`t[]`tâ€¢  Integration of additional surveillance sources: extending the data-source abstraction (Section 3.3.1) to ingest other surveillance feeds â€” for example, ASTERIX-encoded radar data, or the 978 MHz UAT link discussed in Section 2.2 â€” would broaden the system's coverage and enable interesting comparative studies between cooperative (ADS-B) and non-cooperative (radar) surveillance data for the same airspace volume.
0538`t[]`tâ€¢  Refinement of the aircraft-classification logic: augmenting the current heuristic fallback (Section 3.7) with a small, purpose-trained classifier â€” using the TRAINING_MATRIX infrastructure already present in the database schema (Section 3.5) and exposed through the /api/training/* endpoints â€” could reduce the mis-classification rate for aircraft operating outside their typical flight envelope.
0539`t[]`tâ€¢  Deployment as a continuously running, multi-user service: extending the current single-host design to support concurrent access by multiple users â€” potentially including authentication, rate-limiting and horizontal scaling â€” would move the system closer to a production-grade deployment suitable for, for example, departmental or institutional use as a standing demonstration and teaching resource.
0541`t[]`tIn closing, this project has demonstrated that a complete, useful and pedagogically rich aircraft-surveillance system can be built, on a modest budget and within an undergraduate timeframe, by combining a sound understanding of electronics-and-communication fundamentals with careful software engineering. It is hoped that the system, and this report documenting it, will prove useful â€” whether as a working tool, as a teaching aid, or as a foundation for future student projects â€” well beyond the scope of the present submission.

0593`t[]`t    reg = bits_88 << 24
0594`t[]`t    for i in range(87, -1, -1):
0595`t[]`t        if (reg >> (i + 24)) & 1:
0596`t[]`t            reg ^= GENERATOR_POLY << i
0597`t[]`t    return reg & 0xFFFFFF

0600`t[]`t    """Return True if the received PI field matches the"""
0601`t[]`t    """recomputed remainder (interrogator id assumed zero)."""
0602`t[]`t    payload = frame_112_bits >> 24      # first 88 bits (DF+CA+AA+ME)
