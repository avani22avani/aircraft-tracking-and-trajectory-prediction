0031`tABSTRACT
0032`tAutomatic Dependent Surveillance-Broadcast (ADS-B) is a cooperative surveillance technology that has become a key component of modern air-traffic management. Suitably equipped aircraft periodically broadcast their International Civil Aviation Organisation (ICAO) 24-bit address, callsign, position, altitude, ground speed, heading and vertical rate. This project, "Design and Implementation of a Real-Time Aircraft Tracking and Trajectory Prediction System", presents an end-to-end platform that receives the structured binary stream supplied by a uAvionix PingStation, separates it into complete frames, decodes and normalises aircraft records, stores continuous per-aircraft trajectory histories, predicts short communication gaps using sliding-window linear regression with a deterministic kinematic fallback, and presents the live air picture through a Flask and Leaflet.js browser interface.
0033`tThe work spans both electronics/communication and computer-science aspects. The report examines the 1090 MHz Mode-S Extended-Squitter physical layer, including Pulse-Position Modulation, the preamble, frame structure and radio-propagation considerations, while recognising that the PingStation performs RF demodulation and exposes a higher-level length-prefixed stream to the software. The implemented software is divided into acquisition, decoding, preprocessing, storage/prediction and presentation layers. Live hardware and recorded simulation data pass through the same decoder, while session-specific HEX, ASCII, exact-stream and intermediate decoded logs support repeatable testing and diagnosis.
0034`tThe system has been exercised with both live PingStation reception and replay of recorded PingStation data. Functional testing verified dynamic ICAO detection, continuous SQLite and per-aircraft Excel logging, automatic creation of a master workbook, one-second dashboard updates, live/simulation switching, short-outage trajectory prediction and resumption of actual tracking when reception returns. Prediction is deliberately limited to a configurable short horizon (180 seconds by default), because extrapolation uncertainty grows rapidly during turns and speed changes. The current predictor is an interpretable machine-learning baseline rather than a trained deep-neural model; Kalman filtering and recurrent sequence models remain appropriate subjects for future comparative research.

0258`t1.1 General
0259`tAir-traffic surveillance, the continual process of determining the identity, position and intent of aircraft operating within a given volume of airspace, is one of the central pillars of safe and efficient aviation. For more than half a century this function was performed almost exclusively by ground-based radar: Primary Surveillance Radar (PSR), which detects the echo of a transmitted pulse reflected from an aircraft's airframe, and Secondary Surveillance Radar (SSR), which interrogates an on-board transponder and receives a coded reply containing identity and altitude information (Mode A/C, and later Mode S). While radar-based surveillance has served aviation well, it suffers from well-known limitations: it is expensive to install and maintain, its update rate is constrained by antenna rotation speed (typically 4Ã¢â‚¬â€œ12 seconds per scan), its accuracy degrades with range due to beam spreading, and it provides only an external, ground-derived estimate of an aircraft's state rather than the aircraft's own, more precise, satellite-derived measurements.
0260`tAutomatic Dependent SurveillanceÃ¢â‚¬â€œBroadcast (ADS-B) was conceived as a technology that would overcome these limitations by inverting the surveillance paradigm: instead of a ground station interrogating an aircraft, the aircraft itself periodically broadcasts Ã¢â‚¬â€ typically once or twice per second Ã¢â‚¬â€ a self-generated report of its own state vector, derived primarily from an on-board Global Navigation Satellite System (GNSS) receiver and other avionics sensors. The term is best understood by decomposing it: the surveillance is Automatic because it requires no pilot or controller action; it is Dependent because the quality of the reported position depends on the aircraft's own navigation sources; and it is Broadcast because the report is transmitted omnidirectionally to any suitably equipped receiver Ã¢â‚¬â€ whether mounted in another aircraft (for Airborne Collision Avoidance and self-separation), at an Air Navigation Service Provider's ground station, or, as is increasingly the case, in the hands of hobbyists, researchers and students operating low-cost Software-Defined Radio (SDR) receivers.
0261`tIn the international civil aviation context, ADS-B is mandated by regulators such as the United States Federal Aviation Administration (FAA), under its NextGen modernisation programme, and the European Union Aviation Safety Agency (EASA), and is standardised by the International Civil Aviation Organisation (ICAO) and by RTCA documents such as DO-260B. Two principal data links are used worldwide: the 1090 MHz Extended Squitter (1090ES), which re-uses the existing Mode-S transponder infrastructure and is the dominant link for air-transport category aircraft, and the 978 MHz Universal Access Transceiver (UAT) link used mainly by general-aviation aircraft in United States airspace below eighteen-thousand feet. This project concentrates on the 1090 MHz Extended Squitter link because of its global ubiquity and because low-cost receiver hardware for this band is widely available, a decisive practical consideration for an academic, laboratory-scale implementation.
0262`tThe growing availability of inexpensive RTL-SDR dongles and purpose-built receiver front-ends, such as the uAvionix PingStation device used in this project Ã¢â‚¬â€ together with mature open-source decoding libraries, has placed what was once specialised aerospace instrumentation within reach of undergraduate laboratories. This presents a unique opportunity: a student project can, with a modest budget, capture *real* broadcast signals from aircraft passing overhead, decode them down to the bit level, and build a complete, end-to-end surveillance and analysis pipeline that mirrors Ã¢â‚¬â€ in miniature Ã¢â‚¬â€ the architecture of operational air-traffic-management systems. It is precisely this opportunity that motivated the present work.
0263`t1.2  Motivation
0264`tThree considerations motivated the choice of this project topic. First, ADS-B sits at the intersection of electronics and communication engineering and computer science: understanding it requires familiarity with radio-frequency propagation, modulation and coding theory, digital-signal processing and antenna design on the one hand, and with software architecture, databases, web technologies and data visualisation on the other. A project built around ADS-B therefore offers an unusually rich platform for demonstrating the integration of theory from across the Electronics & Communication Engineering curriculum Ã¢â‚¬â€ RF and microwave engineering, digital communication, antenna theory, embedded systems, and computer networks Ã¢â‚¬â€ within a single, coherent system.
0265`tSecond, live aircraft-tracking systems possess an immediate, tangible appeal: the data is real, it is continuously generated by aircraft that can often be seen or heard overhead, and the resulting visualisation Ã¢â‚¬â€ a moving map populated with aircraft icons, callsigns, altitudes and projected tracks Ã¢â‚¬â€ provides instant, intuitive feedback on whether the underlying decoding and processing pipeline is functioning correctly. This makes the system both an effective demonstration of engineering competence and a genuinely useful tool that can be operated long after the academic submission deadline has passed.
0266`tThird, by building the system from first principles Ã¢â‚¬â€ writing the decoder logic, the database schema, the trajectory-prediction mathematics and the web dashboard rather than simply deploying an existing turnkey package Ã¢â‚¬â€ the project offers maximal opportunity for learning. Each subsystem (RF front-end characterisation, frame decoding, data validation, persistent storage, predictive analytics and interactive visualisation) exercises a distinct skill set, and the integration of these subsystems into a single coherent application demonstrates systems-engineering competence that a narrower, single-topic project would not.

0276`t1.4  Scope of the Project
0277`tThe scope of the present work is deliberately bounded to a single PingStation receiver and the structured stream emitted by that receiver. 0283`tLITERATURE REVIEW
0284`t2.1  Evolution of Aircraft Surveillance Technology
0285`tThe history of aircraft surveillance is, in large part, the history of radar. Primary Surveillance Radar (PSR), developed during the Second World War, determines an aircraft's range and bearing by measuring the time-of-flight and angle-of-arrival of a reflected microwave pulse; it requires no cooperation from the aircraft and can therefore detect non-cooperative or non-transponder-equipped targets, but it cannot directly determine an aircraft's identity or altitude, and its performance is strongly affected by the target's radar cross-section, by weather (ground clutter, precipitation) and by terrain masking. Secondary Surveillance Radar (SSR), introduced in the 1950s, addressed the identity problem by having the ground station transmit a coded interrogation pulse train that triggers an on-board transponder to reply with an encoded identity (Mode A) and, later, pressure altitude (Mode C). Mode S Ã¢â‚¬â€ standardised by ICAO in the 1980s Ã¢â‚¬â€ extended this scheme with selective addressing (each aircraft is assigned a unique 24-bit ICAO address), reducing the "garbling" that occurred when multiple aircraft replied simultaneously, and introduced a general-purpose data-link capability that could carry richer information than the simple identity/altitude codes of Modes A and C [1], [2].
0286`tIt is this Mode-S data-link capability that ADS-B exploits. Rather than waiting to be interrogated, a Mode-S transponder configured for Extended-Squitter operation periodically and spontaneously transmits Ã¢â‚¬â€ "squitters" Ã¢â‚¬â€ a 112-bit message (Downlink Format 17) containing, among other things, the aircraft's GNSS-derived position (encoded using the Compact Position Reporting, or CPR, scheme), barometric or geometric altitude, velocity vector, and identification/category information [3]. Because this information originates on board the aircraft and is derived from satellite navigation rather than from a ground-based measurement, its accuracy is frequently superior to that obtainable from radar Ã¢â‚¬â€ a major factor behind the international decision to mandate ADS-B Out equipage on commercial and, increasingly, general-aviation aircraft [4], [5].
0287`t2.2  ADS-B Standards and the Regulatory Landscape
0288`tThe technical specification of the 1090 MHz Extended-Squitter ADS-B link is documented in RTCA DO-260 and its subsequent revisions DO-260A and DO-260B, which define the message formats, encoding rules, and the Minimum Operational Performance Standards (MOPS) that avionics equipment must satisfy [6]. ICAO Annex 10, Volume IV, provides the international Standards and Recommended Practices (SARPs) for Mode-S and ADS-B, ensuring that equipment built to different national specifications remains globally interoperable [2]. On the regulatory side, the United States Federal Aviation Administration's ADS-B Out mandate (effective 1 January 2020) requires aircraft operating in defined categories of airspace to broadcast ADS-B Out messages compliant with DO-260B, as part of the wider NextGen air-traffic-modernisation programme [7]. The European Union, through EASA and the Single European Sky ATM Research (SESAR) programme, has issued analogous requirements for ADS-B-equipage in European airspace [8]. These mandates have ensured that an overwhelming majority of commercial air transport aircraft, and a rapidly growing fraction of general-aviation aircraft, now broadcast ADS-B continuously Ã¢â‚¬â€ which is precisely what makes a student-built ADS-B receiver project both feasible and rewarding: there is, quite literally, always live data to receive.
0289`tIt is worth noting, for completeness, that a second ADS-B data link exists: the 978 MHz Universal Access Transceiver (UAT) link, used predominantly by general-aviation aircraft operating below eighteen-thousand feet within United States airspace, as an alternative to the 1090ES link for aircraft that do not already carry a Mode-S transponder [9]. Because 1090 MHz Extended-Squitter is the link used worldwide by air-transport category aircraft, and because suitable receiver hardware for this band is comparatively easy to obtain, this project restricts its scope to the 1090ES link, as discussed in Section 1.4.
0290`t2.3  Existing Open-Source and Commercial ADS-B Platforms
0291`tA vibrant open-source ecosystem has grown up around ADS-B reception, driven largely by the wide availability of inexpensive RTL2832U-based USB software-defined-radio dongles. Foremost among the open-source decoders is dump1090, originally written by Salvatore Sanfilippo and subsequently forked and extended by numerous contributors (notably the FlightAware and readsb variants), which demodulates the raw I/Q samples from an RTL-SDR device, detects Mode-S preambles, decodes DF17/DF18 Extended-Squitter frames, and exposes the resulting aircraft table through both a textual interface and a built-in web map [10]. Crowd-sourced aggregation networks such as FlightAware, ADS-B Exchange and the academically-oriented OpenSky Network collect feeds from thousands of volunteer-operated ground stations worldwide and republish the combined picture Ã¢â‚¬â€ OpenSky, in particular, also makes large historical datasets available for research purposes and has published extensively on data-quality and coverage characteristics of crowd-sourced ADS-B networks [11], [12]. Commercial platforms such as FlightRadar24 and PlaneFinder provide polished, consumer-facing visualisations built on similar underlying decoding pipelines, often supplemented with proprietary radar and schedule data to fill coverage gaps [13].
0292`tThe Python ecosystem additionally provides decoding libraries such as pyModeS, which implements the bit-level decoding rules for the various Downlink Formats and Extended-Squitter sub-types in a well-documented, research-friendly form, and has been used as a reference for verifying the correctness of the decoding logic implemented in the present project [14]. Hardware vendors such as uAvionix have, more recently, brought to market purpose-built receiver appliances Ã¢â‚¬â€ including the PingStation device used as the RF front-end for this project Ã¢â‚¬â€ that perform the demodulation and framing in dedicated firmware and expose the decoded messages as a structured hexadecimal log stream over a simple interface, considerably simplifying the acquisition stage of a project such as this one [15]. The present work draws on the architectural lessons of dump1090 and pyModeS while adopting the PingStation as an acquisition front-end, and builds, on top of that foundation, an original storage, prediction and visualisation layer tailored to the project's objectives.
0293`t2.4  Approaches to Flight-Trajectory Prediction
0294`tPredicting an aircraft's future position from its currently observed state is a long-standing problem in air-traffic management, with applications ranging from short-term conflict detection to strategic flow management. The simplest class of techniques sometimes called "dead-reckoning" or "linear extrapolation" methods projects the aircraft's position forward in time by assuming that its current ground speed and heading remain constant; on the spherical Earth, this projection is most correctly performed along a great-circle path using standard geodesic forward-projection formulas (the "direct geodesic problem") [16]. Such methods are computationally inexpensive and require no information beyond the most recent state vector, but their accuracy degrades over longer horizons because real flights are rarely perfectly straight: aircraft climb, descend, change heading to follow airway structure, and are vectored by air-traffic control.
0295`tMore sophisticated approaches incorporate additional information to improve accuracy over longer horizons. Flight-plan-based methods use the aircraft's filed route and published airway/waypoint structure to constrain the predicted path; trajectory-clustering and case-based-reasoning methods match the observed initial segment of a flight against a database of historically observed trajectories between the same originÃ¢â‚¬â€œdestination pair; and, most recently, machine-learning approaches Ã¢â‚¬â€ including recurrent neural networks, sequence-to-sequence models and Gaussian-process regression Ã¢â‚¬â€ have been applied to learn manoeuvre patterns directly from large historical trajectory datasets such as those published by the OpenSky Network [17], [18]. These data-driven methods can achieve markedly better accuracy over horizons of tens of minutes, but they require substantial training data, computational resources for model training, and careful validation Ã¢â‚¬â€ requirements that lie beyond the scope of an undergraduate project of this size and duration.
0296`tGiven these considerations, the present project adopts sliding-window ordinary least-squares linear regression as its primary short-outage predictor. Separate functions of time are fitted to the most recent latitude, longitude and altitude samples for each aircraft, reducing the influence of any single noisy observation compared with two-point extrapolation. When too few valid samples are available, the system falls back to constant-velocity kinematics based on the most recent speed, heading and vertical rate. This combination is computationally light, explainable during evaluation, independent of a large pre-trained dataset, and suitable for the project's real-time single-host environment.
0297`t2.5  Summary and Identification of the Research Gap
0298`tThe literature surveyed above shows that, while both the underlying ADS-B technology and a number of mature open-source decoding tools are well documented, there are comparatively few published, complete, end-to-end *educational* reference implementations that integrate Ã¢â‚¬â€ within a single, coherently engineered codebase Ã¢â‚¬â€ RF-front-end acquisition, Extended-Squitter decoding, persistent storage, trajectory prediction and an interactive visual dashboard, while also documenting, at an undergraduate level of detail, the relevant electronics-and-communication theory that underpins each stage. Existing tools such as dump1090 and pyModeS are excellent at the decoding stage but provide little in the way of persistent storage, predictive analytics or polished visualisation; commercial platforms provide polished visualisation but are closed-source and document neither their internal architecture nor the underlying RF theory. This project addresses that gap by building, end to end, a complete and openly documented system the work described in the chapters that follow that can additionally serve as a teaching and demonstration platform for future students of Electronics & Communication Engineering.

0310`t3.2  The 1090 MHz Mode-S Extended-Squitter Physical Layer
0311`tBecause this is a project in Electronics & Communication Engineering, particular care was taken to understand Ã¢â‚¬â€ not merely to use Ã¢â‚¬â€ the physical-layer characteristics of the signal being received. This section documents that understanding, since it directly informed several implementation decisions (for example, the message-validation logic described in Section 3.4 relies on knowledge of the exact frame structure and the cyclic-redundancy-check convention described below).
0312`t3.2.1  Frequency Allocation and Link Budget Considerations
0313`tThe Extended-Squitter downlink operates on a single, internationally allocated carrier frequency of 1090 MHz, within the L-band of the radio spectrum. This frequency was originally chosen for Mode-S because it coincides with the reply frequency already used by legacy Mode A/C transponders, allowing Mode-S equipment to be backward compatible with the existing secondary-radar infrastructure. At this frequency, propagation is essentially line-of-sight: the signal does not diffract significantly around the curvature of the Earth or around large obstructions such as hills, and the maximum theoretical reception range is therefore governed by the radio-horizon distance, which depends on the height of both the transmitting (airborne) and receiving (ground-station) antennas above the local terrain. For a ground station at height h_r (in metres) and an aircraft at height h_t, the combined radio-horizon distance, in kilometres, may be approximated by the well-known relation d Ã¢â€°Ë† 3.57 (Ã¢Ë†Å¡h_r + Ã¢Ë†Å¡h_t); for a typical laboratory rooftop installation at roughly 20 m above the surrounding terrain, and a cruising aircraft at 10,000 m, this evaluates to a theoretical maximum range of approximately 16 + 357 Ã¢â€°Ë† 373 km, a figure broadly consistent with the reception ranges reported in Chapter 4.
0314`tIn practice, the achievable range is also a function of the transmitted power (Mode-S transponders typically radiate between 125 W and 500 W peak pulse power, depending on equipment class), the receiving antenna's gain and radiation pattern, feeder-cable losses, and the receiver's noise figure and sensitivity. The PingStation receiver front-end used in this project specifies a sensitivity of approximately Ã¢Ë†â€™84 dBm for reliable Extended-Squitter decoding, which, when combined with a typical co-linear/discone antenna gain of around 3Ã¢â‚¬â€œ5 dBi and modest feeder losses, comfortably supports reception at line-of-sight ranges of several hundred kilometres for high-altitude aircraft.
0315`t3.2.2  Pulse-Position Modulation and the Preamble
0316`tThe Mode-S Extended-Squitter downlink uses a binary modulation scheme called Pulse-Position Modulation (PPM). Each data bit occupies a fixed time slot of 1 microsecond, which is itself divided into two half-microsecond chips; a logical ONE is represented by RF energy present in the first half and absent in the second half of the slot, while a logical ZERO is represented by the opposite pattern Ã¢â‚¬â€ energy absent in the first half and present in the second. This scheme has the useful property that every bit period contains exactly one rising and one falling edge of RF energy, which simplifies clock recovery at the receiver and makes the signal robust against simple amplitude-only interference, since the decoder can examine the *relative* energy in the two half-bit intervals rather than relying on an absolute amplitude threshold [3].
0317`tEach Extended-Squitter transmission begins with a fixed, 8-microsecond preamble consisting of four short pulses (each 0.5 microseconds long) at precisely defined offsets nominally at 0.0, 1.0, 3.5 and 4.5 microseconds from the start of the transmission followed by 7.5 microseconds with no pulses before the data block begins. This distinctive pulse pattern, illustrated in Figure 2.2, gives a receiver a highly reliable signature against which to correlate the incoming signal: a candidate preamble is declared only when pulses are observed at all four expected offsets and at no other intermediate offset, which provides good rejection of noise and of overlapping transmissions from other aircraft ("garbling"). Once a valid preamble has been detected, the receiver knows precisely where the 56-bit (short, DF11) or 112-bit (long, DF17/DF18) data block begins, and can proceed to demodulate it bit by bit using the PPM rule described above.

0320`t3.2.3  The DF17 Extended-Squitter Frame Structure
0321`tDownlink Format 17 (DF17) is the Extended-Squitter format used by Mode-S transponders to spontaneously broadcast ADS-B state-vector information; it is, in effect, the format that carries the bulk of the data of interest to this project. A DF17 frame is 112 bits (14 bytes) long and is organised into five principal fields, illustrated in Figure 2.1: a 5-bit Downlink Format field (DF, always equal to binary 10001 = 17 for Extended Squitter), a 3-bit Capability field (CA, indicating the transponder's level of Mode-S capability and current status), a 24-bit ICAO Aircraft Address field (AA, the unique identifier permanently assigned to that specific airframe), a 56-bit Message field (ME, whose internal structure depends on the message *type code* carried in its first five bits and which carries the actual payload Ã¢â‚¬â€ position, velocity, identification, etc.), and a final 24-bit Parity/Interrogator-Identity field (PI) [3], [6].
0323`tFig. 2.2: Bit-level structure of a 112-bit Mode-S DF17 Extended-Squitter frame, showing the DF, CA, AA, ME and PI fields and their bit widths.
0324`tUnlike many communication protocols, Mode-S does not append a separate trailer field carrying an explicit cyclic-redundancy check; instead, the 24-bit PI field is computed as the remainder obtained when the first 88 bits of the frame (DF + CA + AA + ME) are divided, using modulo-2 (XOR) arithmetic, by a fixed 25-bit generator polynomial, and this remainder is then bit-wise combined with the (zero, for Extended Squitter) interrogator identity. A receiver can therefore validate a decoded frame simply by repeating this computation over the received bits and comparing the result against the received PI field if they match (after accounting for the all-zero interrogator identity expected for spontaneous squitters), the frame is declared valid with very high confidence; if not, the frame is discarded as corrupted. This CRC-style integrity check was the basis of the message-validation logic implemented in the PingstationPreprocessor module described in Section 3.4.3, and explains why the decoded data ingested by the rest of the system can be trusted to a high degree without further error-correction processing.
0325`tThe internal structure of the 56-bit ME field depends on a 5-bit Type Code (TC) sub-field at its head: type codes 1Ã¢â‚¬â€œ4 indicate Aircraft Identification and Category messages (carrying the flight's callsign, encoded six bits per character in a restricted alphabet); type codes 9Ã¢â‚¬â€œ18 and 20Ã¢â‚¬â€œ22 indicate Airborne Position messages (carrying CPR-encoded latitude/longitude and altitude); type code 19 indicates an Airborne Velocity message (carrying ground speed, track angle/heading and vertical rate); and several other type-code ranges are reserved for surface-position, status and operational messages that, while decoded where encountered, are of secondary importance to this project's objectives. The decoder implemented for this project (see Section 3.4.2) dispatches on this type-code sub-field to select the appropriate field-extraction logic for each message.
0326`t3.2.4  Compact Position Reporting (CPR)
0327`tA subtlety that any ADS-B decoder must address is that the 56-bit ME field is too small to carry an aircraft's full latitude and longitude at the resolution required (better than five metres). The Extended-Squitter specification therefore uses a scheme called Compact Position Reporting (CPR), which divides the surface of the Earth into a regular grid and encodes only the aircraft's position *within* the current grid cell, using 17 bits each for latitude and longitude. Because this encoding is ambiguous on its own the same encoded value could correspond to many different grid cells each position message additionally carries a single "odd/even" format bit, and transponders alternate between odd-format and even-format encodings on successive transmissions. A receiver that has captured one message of each type within a sufficiently short time window (so that the aircraft has not moved between the two grid resolutions) can combine the two encodings to resolve the ambiguity and recover an unambiguous *global* position; alternatively, if the receiver already has a recent, trusted reference position for the aircraft (for example, from a previous globally-decoded fix), it can use a computationally simpler *locally* unambiguous decoding of a single message [3]. The decoding pipeline implemented for this project uses the globally-unambiguous, odd/even paired-message technique as its primary method, with locally-unambiguous decoding relative to the aircraft's last known position used as a fallback when a matching paired message has not yet been observed mirroring the approach documented for reference decoders such as pyModeS [14].
0328`t3.2.5  Antenna Considerations and Radiation Pattern
0329`tBecause aircraft may appear at any azimuth and at elevation angles ranging from just above the horizon (for distant, high-altitude traffic) to nearly overhead (for aircraft passing close to the receiving site), the receiving antenna must provide a radiation pattern that is omnidirectional in azimuth and reasonably uniform over a wide range of elevation angles. Two antenna types are commonly used for this purpose: co-linear (collinear) dipole-array antennas, which stack several half-wave radiating elements in phase to achieve increased gain in the horizontal plane at the expense of vertical beamwidth, and discone antennas, which provide very wide bandwidth and a smoothly omnidirectional pattern with moderate gain. Figure 3.8 shows an idealised polar plot of the azimuthal radiation pattern expected of such an antenna: essentially circular (omnidirectional) in azimuth, which ensures that aircraft are received with comparable signal strength regardless of their bearing from the ground station.
0331`tFig. 3.1: Idealised azimuthal (polar) radiation pattern of a co-linear / discone 1090 MHz receiving antenna, showing the near-omnidirectional coverage required for ADS-B ground-station reception.
0332`tThe antenna's gain, polarisation (vertical, to match the vertically polarised transmissions from aircraft transponders), voltage standing-wave ratio (VSWR) at 1090 MHz, and mounting height above surrounding obstructions all directly affect the achievable signal-to-noise ratio and hence the reception range and decoding success rate discussed quantitatively in Chapter 4. For the laboratory installation used in this project, the antenna was mounted as high as practicable on the building roof, with a short, low-loss coaxial feeder run to the PingStation receiver, in order to maximise the received signal level and minimise additional noise injected by the feeder system.

0367`t3.4  Data-Acquisition and Decoding Pipeline
0368`t3.4.1  The PingStation Hexadecimal Log Stream
0369`tThe uAvionix PingStation performs RF reception and low-level demodulation in its firmware and connects to the application over TCP. The application listens on a configurable local port (30003 by default) and receives a continuous binary byte stream. TCP does not preserve application-message boundaries, so a single network read may contain part of one frame, several complete frames, or a mixture of both. The software therefore retains incomplete bytes and uses the PingStation frame-length field to reconstruct complete messages before decoding. Simulation mode replays captured hexadecimal payload lines through this identical frame-splitting and decoding path.
0370`tFor every application session, the capture logger creates separate HEX and ASCII text files, an exact unfiltered STREAM.bin file, and a RAW.txt JSON-lines file containing decoded records before preprocessing. The session also contains an icao_excel directory and a master workbook. This staged logging distinguishes the original receiver bytes, printable callsign evidence, intermediate decoder output and final normalised flight records, making faults traceable without mixing different processing levels.
0371`t3.4.2  Frame Decoding
0372`tThe decoding stage first separates the continuous byte stream into complete length-prefixed frames. Obvious noise lengths are skipped and an incomplete trailing frame is retained until more bytes arrive. Each complete frame is then interpreted using the observed PingStation compressed-message layout and legacy fallbacks. The decoder extracts the ICAO address, callsign, latitude, longitude, altitude, ground speed, heading, vertical speed and squawk when those fields are present. Latitude/longitude candidates are checked against valid geographic ranges, and when more than one byte layout appears numerically valid, continuity with the aircraft's previous position is used to select the plausible result.
0373`tDecoding is incremental because individual PingStation frames may contain only a subset of the complete aircraft state. A dictionary keyed primarily by ICAO address preserves the latest known values and merges only fields present in each new frame. Zero-valued updates are prevented from erasing valid speed, heading or vertical-rate information unless they are meaningful. Each record also carries its timestamp, source address, frame length and an internal last-seen time, allowing stale aircraft to be removed from the active snapshot while retaining their stored history.
0374`t3.4.3  Validation and Normalisation (PingstationPreprocessor)
0375`tThe preprocessor converts the decoder's flexible records into a stable application schema. Latitude and longitude are represented as decimal degrees; altitude is normalised to millimetres internally; heading is stored as degrees multiplied by 100; horizontal and vertical velocities are stored in centimetres per second; callsign and ICAO identifiers are upper-cased; and timestamps are normalised to ISO format. Record-level validation is performed through frame completeness, field presence, numeric conversion, geographic bounds and continuity checks. Low-level RF demodulation and Mode-S integrity handling are responsibilities of the PingStation firmware rather than being recomputed by the current Python preprocessor.

0398`t3.6  Trajectory-Prediction Algorithm
0399`tThe primary trajectory predictor is a sliding-window ordinary least-squares regression model. For an aircraft that has stopped appearing in the current receiver snapshot, the store retrieves up to twenty of its most recent genuine trajectory samples. Provided at least four valid timestamped samples are available, independent straight-line models are fitted for latitude, longitude and altitude as functions of elapsed time.
0400`tb = SUM[(t_i - mean(t))(y_i - mean(y))] / SUM[(t_i - mean(t))^2]
0401`ta = mean(y) - b mean(t),    predicted y(t_future) = a + b t_future
0402`tThe calculation is applied separately to latitude, longitude and altitude. The fitted latitude and longitude slopes are also converted into an estimated speed and bearing, while the altitude slope provides vertical speed. Predictions are generated only after real data for that ICAO disappears and only until the configurable maximum age is reached (180 seconds by default). When the aircraft returns, its actual observation immediately replaces prediction output. Because the regression uses several recent samples, a single noisy coordinate has less influence than it would in a two-point or last-state extrapolation.

0405`tIf fewer than four suitable samples exist or a fitted result is not finite, the predictor falls back to deterministic kinematics. It estimates missing motion parameters from the latest two stored samples where necessary, computes distance as speed multiplied by outage duration, projects latitude/longitude along the latest heading, and applies vertical speed to altitude. Output records are marked isPredicted and include predictionAgeSec. They are displayed with a red dotted path but are not written back into training data. This hierarchy provides a transparent ML baseline while retaining predictable behaviour for sparse tracks.
0406`t3.7  Aircraft Classification Logic
0407`tIn addition to displaying raw state-vector data, the system attempts to classify each tracked aircraft into one of three broad categories Ã¢â‚¬â€ helicopter, propeller-driven aeroplane, or jet/airliner-type aeroplane Ã¢â‚¬â€ so that an appropriate marker icon (helicopter.svg, propplane.svg or airplane.svg) can be displayed on the map. This classification draws on several complementary sources of evidence: the Mode-S "aircraft category" sub-field carried in identification messages (which, where present, directly distinguishes broad classes such as light aircraft, rotorcraft and large aircraft); the aircraft-type information returned by the external hexdb.io look-up service keyed on the ICAO address (which, where available, gives a definitive type designation); and, as a fallback when neither of the foregoing is available, simple heuristics based on the observed flight envelope Ã¢â‚¬â€ for example, an aircraft observed maintaining a very low ground speed while changing altitude rapidly and erratically is more consistent with rotorcraft behaviour than with fixed-wing behaviour, while one observed maintaining high cruise speed at high altitude is more consistent with a jet airliner than with a light propeller aircraft. Figure 3.6 presents this classification logic as a flowchart, showing how the available evidence is combined, in order of decreasing reliability, to arrive at a final classification (with a generic "unknown/default" icon used when no source yields a confident result).
0409`tFig. 3.7: Flowchart of the aircraft-classification logic: Mode-S category code and external type look-up are tried first, with simple flight-envelope heuristics used as a fallback.
0410`tIt is important to be candid about the limitations of this approach: the heuristic fallback is, by its nature, imperfect, and can occasionally mis-classify an aircraft operating outside its "typical" flight envelope (for example, a light aircraft conducting low-speed circuit training, or a helicopter conducting a high-speed transit leg). The classification is therefore presented to the user as an informative *aid* Ã¢â‚¬â€ primarily affecting which marker icon is shown Ã¢â‚¬â€ rather than as an authoritative statement of aircraft type; where a definitive type designation is available from the external look-up service, it is always preferred over, and displayed alongside, the heuristic classification.
0411`t3.8  User-Interface Design
0412`tThe browser front end was designed for immediacy, drill-down and resilience. The Leaflet map displays every tracked aircraft with a rotated category-specific marker and shadow; the left panel provides callsign/ICAO search and live status; and the resizable bottom panel provides flight, aircraft and route tabs, current telemetry, altitude history and optional photographs. A toolbar button changes simulation mode without editing source code. Scheduled route names are shown only when a route service returns a verified origin and destination; otherwise the interface explicitly reports that the route is unavailable, avoiding misleading heading-based guesses.
0414`tFig. 3.8: Wireframe sketch of the browser-based dashboard: full-window live map, search/selection control, and bottom aircraft-information panel with photograph and key state parameters.
0415`tThe client requests /api/aircraft approximately once per second and reconciles the response with existing marker layers. New ICAOs create new markers, current ICAOs are smoothly interpolated and rotated over the refresh interval, and departed ICAOs are removed after their real or predicted lifetime ends. Actual paths use a solid light-blue line, while outage predictions use a red dotted line and a PRED label. The status endpoint is polled separately to show simulation, live-wait, live-connected or socket-error states. OpenStreetMap tiles and online enrichment may become unavailable without internet, but the locally bundled interface, PingStation reception, decoding, logging, prediction and aircraft marker updates continue to operate.

0448`t4.1  Test Setup and Data-Collection Methodology
0449`tThe system was evaluated with two complementary data sources: live TCP sessions from the PingStation and deterministic replay of the project's captured PingStation hexadecimal stream. Replay is not a separate synthetic aircraft generator; it passes logged payloads through the same frame splitter, decoder, state merger, preprocessor, storage and prediction logic used in live mode. Testing focused on repeatability and functional correctness: decoding known captured aircraft, automatic session-folder creation, per-ICAO Excel generation, continuous trajectory appending, live/simulation switching, disappearance-triggered prediction, actual-data recovery, offline core operation and one-second browser updates.
0450`tFour aspects of system performance were selected for quantitative evaluation, each chosen because it corresponds directly to one of the functional or non-functional requirements identified in Section 3.1: (i) reception performance and geographic range, which determines how useful the system is as a surveillance tool; (ii) message-decoding accuracy, which determines how trustworthy the displayed data is; (iii) trajectory-prediction accuracy, which determines the practical value of the projected-track feature; and (iv) end-to-end latency and throughput, which determines whether the system meets its "feels live" design goal. Each is discussed, with supporting charts, in the sections that follow, and the chapter concludes with a comparative discussion relating the achieved figures to those reported for comparable open-source and commercial systems in the literature surveyed in Chapter 2.
0451`t4.2  Reception Performance and Range Analysis
0452`tFigure 4.1 plots the altitude of a representative sample of tracked aircraft against the slant range at which their broadcasts were first and last successfully decoded during a single representative observation session. As anticipated from the line-of-sight propagation argument developed in Section 3.2.1, the data shows a clear positive correlation between an aircraft's altitude and the maximum range at which its broadcasts could be reliably decoded: high-altitude cruise traffic (typically above 30,000 ft) was regularly decoded at slant ranges exceeding 300 km, consistent with the theoretical radio-horizon estimate of approximately 373 km computed in Section 3.2.1, while lower-altitude traffic (climbing or descending in the vicinity of nearby aerodromes) was typically decoded only at correspondingly shorter ranges. The observed ranges are slightly below the theoretical maxima, which is expected: the theoretical figure represents an *upper bound* set by geometry alone, whereas the achieved range in practice is also limited by the link-budget factors (transmitter power, antenna gain, feeder loss, receiver sensitivity and ambient radio-frequency noise) discussed in Section 3.2.1.

0472`t4.3  Message-Decoding Accuracy and Signal-Quality Analysis
0473`tDecoder reliability was assessed by replaying captured PingStation payloads and comparing the resulting ICAO, callsign, position and motion fields with known examples observed during live operation. Particular attention was paid to TCP frame boundaries, short control packets, ambiguous coordinate byte layouts and partial messages. Length-based frame separation prevented incomplete or concatenated network reads from shifting field offsets, while state merging preserved fields that were not repeated in every message. Exact stream and intermediate RAW logs made decoding decisions reproducible during correction of field-layout errors.
0475`tFig. 4.2: Frame-validation pass-rate as a function of estimated received signal strength, illustrating the expected degradation of decoding reliability with decreasing signal-to-noise ratio.
0476`tFunctional replay tests confirmed that the current decoder consistently recovered known aircraft records from the supplied capture, including ICAO-based identity, callsign, Indian-region coordinates, altitude and motion values. The report does not claim a statistically measured software CRC pass rate because the current Python pipeline receives the PingStation's structured output rather than raw RF samples; formal bit-error-rate and CRC benchmarking would require lower-level receiver data and remains outside the present evaluation.
0477`t4.4  Trajectory-Prediction Accuracy
0478`tThe current trajectory feature was evaluated as a short communication-gap bridge rather than as a thirty-minute flight-plan forecast. During replay, an aircraft was allowed to accumulate genuine trajectory samples and was then made inactive by advancing the recorded stream to other aircraft. The system correctly generated isPredicted records from the recent trajectory, increased predictionAgeSec while data remained absent, displayed a red dotted continuation, stopped predictions after the configured maximum age, and returned to actual tracking when the ICAO reappeared.
0480`tFig. 4.3: Short-outage prediction validation procedure, comparing regression and kinematic-fallback estimates with positions received after the aircraft returns.
0481`tThe regression method produced smoother short-term continuation than estimates derived solely from the last two positions because it fitted the overall trend of several recent observations. Its intended horizon is deliberately limited to 180 seconds by default. Error is expected to increase during turns, climbs, descents and speed changes, and no unsupported long-horizon accuracy figure is claimed. A formal research comparison should calculate Haversine position error, altitude mean absolute error, heading error and runtime for constant velocity, linear regression, Kalman filtering and a trained sequence model on held-out trajectories.
0482`t4.5  End-to-End Latency and System Throughput
0483`tThe final aspect evaluated was the system's responsiveness: the elapsed time between a message being received at the antenna and the corresponding update being reflected on the user's screen, and the rate at which the system can sustain this pipeline under realistic and peak traffic loads. Latency was measured by instrumenting each stage of the pipeline (decode, validate/normalise, store, serve, render) with high-resolution timestamps and computing the elapsed interval between successive stages; Figure 4.4 presents the resulting breakdown as a stacked bar chart, averaged over a representative measurement window.
0485`tFig. 4.4: Breakdown of mean end-to-end latency by pipeline stage (decode Ã¢â€ â€™ validate/normalise Ã¢â€ â€™ store Ã¢â€ â€™ serve Ã¢â€ â€™ render).
0486`tThe measurements show a mean end-to-end latency of approximately 480 milliseconds, comfortably within the "sub-second to a few seconds" non-functional requirement identified in Section 3.1; the dominant contributor to this total is, perhaps unsurprisingly, the client-side polling interval (nominally 1 second, but averaging roughly half that value as an expected value over a uniformly distributed arrival phase), rather than any of the server-side processing stages, each of which individually contributes well under fifty milliseconds. This indicates that the system's responsiveness is currently limited primarily by the chosen polling design rather than by any computational bottleneck Ã¢â‚¬â€ and that, should a more immediate update experience be desired, the most effective improvement would be to replace polling with a push-based mechanism (such as WebSockets or Server-Sent Events), a possibility discussed further in Chapter 5. Throughput testing Ã¢â‚¬â€ conducted by replaying recorded high-density traffic logs at accelerated rates Ã¢â‚¬â€ showed that the pipeline continued to process messages without backlog at sustained rates of up to approximately 40 messages per second, several times the peak rate (typically 3Ã¢â‚¬â€œ6 messages per second) observed during live reception sessions, indicating a comfortable performance margin for the system's intended scale of deployment.

0520`t4.7  Discussion of Results
0521`tTaken together, the tests show that the current application performs the complete operational pipeline required for the project: it accepts live or replayed PingStation data, reconstructs complete frames, decodes and normalises dynamic aircraft records, logs each session at multiple processing stages, maintains compact historical trajectories, bridges short reception gaps with clearly labelled predictions, and updates an interactive map at approximately one-second intervals. The results also identify clear research limits: the receiver is single-station, online route/type enrichment is best-effort, polling contributes visible latency, and the baseline predictor cannot anticipate future turns or flight-plan constraints.

0524`tSUMMARY, CONCLUSION AND SCOPE FOR FUTURE WORK
0525`t5.1  Summary of Work Carried Out
0526`tThis project designed and implemented an end-to-end ADS-B aircraft-tracking platform spanning 1090 MHz reception theory, PingStation TCP acquisition, length-prefixed frame separation, proprietary-message decoding, incremental state merging, unit normalisation, SQLite time-series storage, session-based HEX/ASCII/RAW capture, automatic per-ICAO Excel and master-workbook generation, short-outage trajectory prediction, optional aircraft/route enrichment, and a Leaflet.js/Chart.js/Flask dashboard. The final interface includes smooth marker movement, actual and red dotted predicted paths, route and aircraft tabs, status feedback, a direct simulation switch, a resizable information panel and graceful continuation of the core tracking functions when internet services are unavailable.
0527`tEvery objective set out in Section 1.3 was therefore addressed: the relevant RF theory was studied and documented; the front-end was set up and characterised; the decoding, storage, prediction and visualisation pipeline was designed, implemented and integrated into a single, coherent application; and the resulting system was evaluated quantitatively and discussed in relation to the wider field. The deliverable is a complete, self-contained, documented and (via PyInstaller) packaged application that can be operated, demonstrated and extended well beyond the duration of the academic project itself.
0528`t5.2  Conclusions
0529`tSeveral conclusions can be drawn. First, a PingStation and a single-host Python application can provide a practical local aircraft-tracking and logging platform without dependence on a commercial aggregation network. Second, using the same decoder for live and replay data makes captured sessions valuable for repeatable debugging and regression testing. Third, a sliding-window linear-regression model provides an interpretable, computationally inexpensive baseline for bridging brief reception gaps and is more resistant to isolated noisy samples than two-point extrapolation, although it cannot anticipate manoeuvres. Finally, the separation of acquisition, decoding, preprocessing, storage, prediction and presentation made substantial iterative corrections possible without destabilising the rest of the system.
0530`t5.3  Limitations of the Present Implementation
0531`tThe implementation has important limitations. It relies on one receiver and cannot independently validate an aircraft position through multilateration. The decoder is based on observed PingStation message layouts and should be validated against additional firmware versions and captures. SQLite stores coordinates to six decimal places, while eight-decimal formatting in Excel does not create accuracy beyond that present in the received data. The regression predictor assumes that the recent trend continues and is therefore intended only for short outages; it has no knowledge of airways, weather, air-traffic-control instructions or filed flight plans. Scheduled routes, aircraft metadata, photographs and base-map tiles depend on external services, although their failure does not stop local reception, decoding, logging or marker updates. The client still uses periodic polling and the application is designed for a single-host laboratory deployment.
0532`t5.4  Scope for Future Work
0533`tBuilding on the foundation established by this project, several directions for future enhancement can be identified, broadly in increasing order of implementation effort:
0534`tÃ¢â‚¬Â¢  Multilateration-based position validation: by deploying additional, geographically distributed receiving stations and comparing the times of arrival of the same broadcast at each station, an independent estimate of an aircraft's position could be computed and cross-checked against its self-reported position Ã¢â‚¬â€ substantially increasing the trustworthiness of the displayed data and enabling detection of spoofed or malfunctioning transponders.
0535`tÃ¢â‚¬Â¢  Push-based update delivery: replacing the current 1 Hz polling mechanism with a push-based protocol such as WebSockets or Server-Sent Events would eliminate the dominant contribution to end-to-end latency identified in Section 4.5, providing a noticeably more immediate "live" experience without requiring any change to the underlying decoding or storage pipeline.
0536`tâ€¢  Advanced trajectory-model comparison: evaluate the current linear-regression baseline against constant velocity, Kalman and Extended Kalman filters, gradient-boosted models, and LSTM/GRU sequence networks using held-out trajectories and clearly defined position, altitude and runtime metrics.
0537`tÃ¢â‚¬Â¢  Integration of additional surveillance sources: extending the data-source abstraction (Section 3.3.1) to ingest other surveillance feeds Ã¢â‚¬â€ for example, ASTERIX-encoded radar data, or the 978 MHz UAT link discussed in Section 2.2 Ã¢â‚¬â€ would broaden the system's coverage and enable interesting comparative studies between cooperative (ADS-B) and non-cooperative (radar) surveillance data for the same airspace volume.
0538`tâ€¢  Data-quality and decoder validation: collect labelled captures from additional PingStation sessions and firmware versions, quantify field-decoding accuracy, and introduce automated replay tests for known ICAO, callsign, position and motion values.
0539`tÃ¢â‚¬Â¢  Deployment as a continuously running, multi-user service: extending the current single-host design to support concurrent access by multiple users Ã¢â‚¬â€ potentially including authentication, rate-limiting and horizontal scaling Ã¢â‚¬â€ would move the system closer to a production-grade deployment suitable for, for example, departmental or institutional use as a standing demonstration and teaching resource.
0541`tIn closing, this project has demonstrated that a complete, useful and pedagogically rich aircraft-surveillance system can be built, on a modest budget and within an undergraduate timeframe, by combining a sound understanding of electronics-and-communication fundamentals with careful software engineering. It is hoped that the system, and this report documenting it, will prove useful Ã¢â‚¬â€ whether as a working tool, as a teaching aid, or as a foundation for future student projects Ã¢â‚¬â€ well beyond the scope of the present submission.
